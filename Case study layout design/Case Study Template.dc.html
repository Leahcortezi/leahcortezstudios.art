01 / The Brief

Make a dead specimen usable again

Translate a historical metal or wood type specimen into a functional contemporary digital typeface.

The challenge was to preserve the character and visual identity of the original reference while adapting it for modern use — with a strong focus on accessibility, readability, and consistency across a full character set.

That meant deciding early what in the specimen was identity and what was simply a limitation of how it was cut. Sharpness and rhythm were worth keeping. Extreme contrast and narrow apertures were not.

Constraint: the family had to hold at 9pt body copy and at 300pt poster display.

02 / Research & references

3 sources

I analyzed the Surendra specimen for proportion, stroke behavior, and overall rhythm, then set it beside two humanist text faces to see where the historical forms would fight legibility.

My initial direction leaned toward blackletter-inspired sharpness. Set as a paragraph, it collapsed — counters filled in and the rhythm went spiky. I moved into a low-contrast humanist system to support long-form reading and practical everyday use.

Each round was judged by printing a page of body copy rather than by looking at single letters. That kept decisions about spacing and weight honest.

04 / Concept development

Personality without losing clarity

The final structure balances open, legible forms with subtle angular serif details. I extended the historical reference into a complete set — lowercase, numerals, punctuation, and supporting marks — so cohesion held across every glyph category.

To make spacing scale, I built a rule-based kerning workflow that pairs manual refinement with shape-aware logic. It kept typographic color even across the family instead of pair-by-pair guesswork.
